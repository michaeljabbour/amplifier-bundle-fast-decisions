\section{On labelled decisions Jev is the best value; on real reads no judge is safe}\label{sec:judges}

\emph{The question at this stage:} telemetry could not say whether a decision was right, so: on cases where the right
answer is known, which decision model is right most often, at what price and speed? To know whether a decision is
right we need labelled cases. We built two benchmarks and graded every decision model
under the gate the bundle ships: act only when the top probability is at least \MinP\ and leads the runner-up by at
least \MinMargin, within \TimeoutS\,s; otherwise fall back to the host.

\paragraph{Constructed cases.} \NumBase\ judges answered \DevN\ development cases (a screen) and \HoldN\ fresh holdout
cases whose analysis was preregistered, \HoldReps\ times each: file reads, searches, selections and computer-use
questions, including cases with side effects or prompt injections. Two blind reviewers checked the labels (Cohen's
$\kappa = \KappaHoldAB$ with the final labels). On the holdout, Jev was right on \JevHoldAccK\ of \HoldN\ cases at
\$\JevHoldCost\ per million decisions and a p95 of \JevHoldPninetyfive\,ms. GPT-6 Luna (\LunaHoldAccK) and GPT-6.1 Sol
(\SolHoldAccK) were slightly more accurate, but neither difference survived the preregistered Holm correction
(adjusted $p = \LunaHoldRAccPHolm$ and \SolHoldRAccPHolm), and both were slower and dearer (p95
\LunaHoldPninetyfive\ and \SolHoldPninetyfive\,ms). Cloudflare's Clef, tested later and labelled post hoc, matched
Jev's accuracy (\ClHoldClefAcc\ against an in-run Jev \ClHoldJevAcc) at \ClClefCostXLow--\ClClefCostXHigh\X\ the cost
per decision and a p95 of \ClClefPninetyfiveLow--\ClClefPninetyfiveHigh\,ms from our machine.

\paragraph{Real decisions.} Constructed cases can be easier or harder than real ones. A second preregistered study
rebuilt \TrN\ real read-shortcut decisions from earlier sessions, exactly as the in-session judge had seen them.
Reviewers agreed with each other ($\kappa = \TrKappaAB$) much more than with what the host actually did next
($\kappa = \TrKappaOutcome$). The preregistered usefulness rule asked for at least \TrUsefulMinReads\ correct
automatic reads with few misfires. \TrNUseful\ of \NumBase\ judges met it (\cref{fig:reads}). Jev took the most
correct reads (\TrJevReads\ of \TrReadCases) but also made \TrJevWaK\ wrong automatic reads.

\begin{keyidea}
Jev is the best value among the decision models we tested: no accuracy difference from the expensive ones was
detected after the preregistered correction, and it is much cheaper and faster. But on real read decisions no model was safe enough to act on its own, so the read shortcut stays
off. The money, if any, had to come from a bigger decision: which model runs the session.
\end{keyidea}

\paragraph{What this left open} A judge can be accurate and still save nothing. The only decision left that clearly
moves money is which model runs the whole session, and that can only be measured in whole sessions
(\cref{sec:paired}).
