Introducing fast decisions for Amplifier

Most of what you ask a coding assistant is quick work. Some of it is a real investigation. Until now, Amplifier treated both the same way: its largest model, thinking hard, every time.

Fast decisions gives Amplifier a dispatcher. Before the work begins, it makes one quick call on how much horsepower the job needs, then gets out of the way. On everyday tasks, Amplifier now finishes in roughly half to three-fifths of the time for about 40% of the cost, with no change in quality. On hard, real-world bug fixes it stays exactly as reliable as before.

How it works

One question, asked once

At the start of every request the dispatcher asks a single question: is this a quick job or a real investigation? The answer picks both the model and how hard it thinks, and that choice holds for the whole request. Work inside a large project always gets the full model. That's the lesson of the bug-fix results below.

Results

A step up where it's safe

Everyday tasks

On twelve small, realistic jobs (fixing a bug, adding a feature, answering a question about code), the dispatcher sends nearly everything to the faster model and finishes in 0.55× the time for 0.38× the cost, with every task passing its hidden checks. On eight further tasks we never tuned against, it ran at 0.61× the time and 0.39× the cost, faster than simply switching Amplifier to the cheaper model.

Real bug fixes

On ten genuine issues from Django, pytest, SymPy, pylint and xarray, a fix only counts if the project's own tests pass. Here the dispatcher gives work inside a large project the full model, which makes it behave exactly like standard Amplifier: 13 of 20 fixed vs. 14 of 20 in the same batch, at the same speed.

Getting there taught us the most. Starting on the cheaper model and switching up partway looked efficient but was 1.2× slower and 1.4× more expensive. On one Django bug it dropped from fixed three times out of three to once out of three: the cheaper model set off in the wrong direction, and the full model, taking over mid-stream, followed it.

Choosing who makes the call

To compare decision-makers, we showed each one pairs of real issues: one rated a quick fix by human experts and one rated an hour or more of work. Jev picks the harder one 83% of the time in about a fifth of a second. A large open model running on this Mac does slightly better (85%) but takes one to two and a half seconds. Mid-size local models land around 72–76%. The tiny model we started with was no better than a coin flip, and no simple rule based on the wording of a request beat 60%.

Systems

Deciding once, keeping the memory

AI services remember the conversation so far so they can answer quickly. That memory only holds while the setup stays the same. Switch models or thinking levels partway through a task and the service rebuilds its memory from scratch. In our runs that meant re-sending up to about 175,000 words at a time.

  • One decision per request. The dispatcher picks the model and thinking level before the first step and never changes them mid-request.
  • Large projects get the full model. A workspace with more than 300 files is treated as a real investigation, whatever the decision-maker says. That's what keeps bug-fix reliability identical.
  • Everything is recorded. Each decision, and who made it, appears in the live dashboard.

Start-up

The slowdown that isn't the AI

On real bug fixes, the AI's own thinking is 86% of the working time. Most of what remains is work Amplifier does before the first word is sent, and it grows with every add-on and every stored conversation. The largest single item we found is part of Amplifier itself, so the fix helps everyone.

Rigor

How sure we are

Every comparison ran side by side, at the same time, against standard Amplifier and against standard Amplifier simply switched to the cheaper model. The second comparison tells us whether savings come from dispatching or just from a cheaper model. Runs were isolated from each other and from this machine's everyday settings.

The confirmation run: strong effect, not yet confirmed by our own rule.

Before starting it, we wrote down five pass criteria for eight unseen everyday tasks, each repeated three times. Four passed: time 0.61× (95% interval 0.47–0.78), cost 0.39×, quality unchanged, every check green. The fifth, a sign test requiring statistical significance, did not: seven tasks were faster and one tied (8.8 s vs. 8.7 s), giving p = 0.07 against a bar of 0.05. With only eight tasks, that test needs all eight to win. Next time we'll register a larger set.

  • Real bug fixes. Ten issues is a small sample. The recommended setup is identical to standard Amplifier on large projects, so equal results are expected. More issues would sharpen the other comparisons.
  • Decision-makers. 90 human-rated issues and 20 everyday tasks: enough to rank them, not a guarantee for every kind of request.
  • Our own test harness. Along the way we found and fixed silently lost measurements, a broken report step, a stale file lock that made a real fix look empty, and runs that could pick up the wrong version of our code. We believe the numbers here are clean because of those fixes.

Availability

Try it

This version of fast decisions lives on the feat/orchestrator-primary branch of the fast-decisions bundle. Adding the bundle makes the dispatcher Amplifier's control loop, with the private built-in rule deciding. To use Jev or your own model, add one setting to your Amplifier settings file; the bundle's documentation shows the exact lines. The start-up fix is drafted and tested and is going through independent review before it goes to the Amplifier project.

  1. Put the dispatcher in the driver's seat. Only the control loop can choose a model; watching from the side can't change anything.
  2. Decide once per request. Switching mid-task throws away the AI service's memory of the conversation, and on hard problems it can send the work the wrong way.
  3. Quick jobs get the fast model; large projects get the full one. Everyday speed, with no loss of reliability.
  4. Fix start-up at the source. This is where hard tasks can still get faster.

Evaluations ran through Amplifier on a single Apple-silicon Mac, driven through terminal sessions and graded independently. Results can differ with other providers, models, add-ons and machines. Routing requests by difficulty follows published work on model routing (RouteLLM, LMSYS, ICLR 2025). Deciding once per request, to keep the service's memory of the conversation intact, is our addition.
