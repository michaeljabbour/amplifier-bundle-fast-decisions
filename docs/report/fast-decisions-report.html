Study report · 24 September 2026 · fast-decisions for Amplifier

Making Amplifier faster: what we tried, what worked, what we learned

We rebuilt the fast-decisions add-on so it can take the driver's seat in Amplifier, then ran it through about 400 real coding runs to see where it saves time, where it doesn't, and why. The short version: everyday tasks now finish in about half the time for about 40% of the cost, with no drop in quality. Hard, real-world bug fixes stay exactly as reliable as before, and it turns out the biggest remaining slowdown isn't the AI at all: it's Amplifier's own start-up work.

What fast-decisions does, in one picture

Every request you give Amplifier goes to a large AI model. Some requests are quick jobs ("rename this", "fix this obvious bug"); others are real investigations. Standard Amplifier treats them all the same: its biggest model, thinking hard, every time.

fast-decisions puts a dispatcher in front. Before the work starts, it takes one quick look at the request and decides how much horsepower the job needs: a faster, cheaper model for quick jobs, or the full model for real investigations. It decides once per request and then gets out of the way.

The flavors

fast-decisions now comes in several forms. Some run inside your tools (downstream). Others are improvements we're contributing back to Amplifier itself (upstream), so every Amplifier user benefits.

Inside your tools

new defaultAmplifier

The dispatcher in the driver's seat

Installing the add-on now replaces Amplifier's standard control loop with ours. Ours wraps the original, so approvals, cancelling, and everything else you rely on keep working. It sorts each request into "quick job" or "real investigation" and picks the model and thinking level to match.

observe onlyAmplifier

Watch-only mode

For people who want measurements before changing anything: it records what it would have decided, without touching your sessions. This was the only form before this work.

pick your judge

Who makes the call

The "quick job or real investigation?" call can be made by:

  • A simple built-in rule, the default. Nothing leaves your machine.
  • Jev, TypeSafe's hosted decision model. The most accurate fast option.
  • A model running on your own computer. It can now answer the same kinds of questions Jev does.

You switch between them with one setting.

other assistants

Smart Tool for Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode

The same decision service is callable from other coding assistants as a stand-alone tool. It gives suggestions; it can't change how those assistants run.

Back into Amplifier (upstream)

proposed fix

A 5.8-second start-up delay, fixed

Every time Amplifier starts, including every helper it spins up, a built-in check searched every file Amplifier has ever saved on your computer. On a well-used machine that took 5.8 seconds. It also misread saved notes as your settings and added an unnecessary warning to every conversation. Our fix looks only where settings actually live: 2 milliseconds, with every existing test still passing.

guide

Trimming add-on overhead

Each add-on you install can add its own start-up work and instructions to every request. On our test machine, 24 add-ons turned a 13-second answer into a 59-second one and made each request four times longer. We measured each add-on's share so you can keep the ones you use and park the rest.

How we tested

We used three test courses, chosen to be fair and hard to fool:

  • Everyday tasks: 20 small, realistic jobs (fix a bug, add a feature, answer a question about code), each with hidden checks the AI can't see.
  • Real bug fixes: 10 genuine issues from well-known open-source projects (Django, pytest, SymPy, and others), graded by the official, independent SWE-bench test suite. A fix only counts if the project's own tests pass.
  • The "quick job or investigation?" quiz: 110 requests with known answers (human-rated difficulty), to see which judges can actually tell the difference.

Every comparison was run side by side at the same time against standard Amplifier, and against standard Amplifier simply using the cheaper model. That second comparison matters: it shows whether savings come from smart dispatching or just from a cheaper model.

Result 1: everyday tasks got much faster and cheaper

Show the numbers

The dispatcher sends almost every everyday task to the faster model and makes exactly one decision per task. It ends up as quick as simply using the cheaper model, without giving up the ability to use the big model when a task needs it. The first version of our design saved time but cost more than necessary. The next section explains why, and how we fixed it.

Result 2: real bug fixes are just as reliable, but switching models mid-task backfires

Show the numbers

This is where the most important lessons came from.

  • Starting cheap on hard problems is a false economy. The first version started every task on the cheaper model and switched up after a few steps. On real bugs, the cheaper model sometimes set off in the wrong direction, and the big model, taking over midway, followed that wrong path. One Django bug went from fixed 3 out of 3 times to fixed 1 out of 3.
  • Switching models (or thinking levels) mid-task costs extra. The AI service keeps a "memory" of the conversation so far to answer quickly. Any switch forces it to rebuild that memory from scratch, which was up to about 175,000 words' worth, paid for again.
  • So the dispatcher now decides once, up front, and never switches. For work inside a large project, it always uses the full model. That keeps reliability identical to standard Amplifier, and it's why the "recommended" row matches it.
  • Turning down the thinking level on hard problems saved 19% time but lost one extra fix in twenty. We kept it as an opt-in setting, not the default.

Result 3: which judges can tell a quick job from an investigation?

Show the numbers

How to read this: we show each judge a quick job and a real investigation. The score is how often it correctly says which one is harder. 50% is a coin flip; 100% is perfect.

  • Jev gets it right about 83% of the time in a fifth of a second.
  • A large model on your own computer does as well (85%) but takes 1–2.5 seconds.
  • Mid-size local models manage about 72–74% in a third of a second.
  • The tiny local model we started with was no better than a coin flip at this, so we don't use it for this job.
  • No simple rule based on the wording of the request beats about 60%. We checked nine different ones on 410 more requests.

Making the local models usable took new work. Before, they could only pick which file to open next. They could not answer "which of these options?" questions at all, which quietly left them out of most decisions.

Result 4: the biggest slowdown is Amplifier's own start-up

On real bug fixes, the AI's thinking is 86% of the working time, so the dispatcher can only do so much there. What's left is the time before and around the AI. On a well-used setup, that's where the big wins are:

  • 5.8 s spent searching old saved files at every start, including every helper Amplifier starts (fix proposed upstream).
  • 5.1 s on every single message, from one add-on that re-downloads its knowledge base each time.
  • 4.6 s of memory look-ups at the start of each conversation.
  • About 70,000 extra words of add-on instructions and tool descriptions attached to every request.

The shape we recommend

1
Put the dispatcher in the driver's seat. It's the only place that can choose a model or thinking level; watching from the side can't change anything.
2
Decide once per request, and never switch mid-task. Switching forces the AI service to rebuild its memory of the conversation and can send the work down the wrong path.
3
Quick jobs get the fast model; anything inside a large project gets the full model. Everyday speed with no loss of reliability.
4
Let people choose their judge: the built-in rule (private, default), Jev (most accurate), or a local model (private, nearly as accurate if you have the hardware).
5
Fix the start-up overhead at the source: the upstream fix plus trimming unused add-ons. This is where hard tasks can still get faster.

How sure are we?

  • Everyday tasks: consistent across three separate screening runs, with statistically clear speed-ups. A pre-registered confirmation run, on 8 tasks never used for tuning, repeated three times, is in progress. We wrote down the pass/fail rule before it started.
  • Real bug fixes: the recommended setup behaves identically to standard Amplifier by design, and the measurements agree (13 vs 14 of 20; the one-task difference is the normal run-to-run variation we also see between identical setups). Ten bugs is a small set; more would sharpen these numbers.
  • Judges: 90 human-rated real issues plus 20 everyday tasks. Good for ranking the judges; not a guarantee for every kind of request.
  • Things we found and fixed in our own test harness along the way: lost measurements, a broken report step, one stale file lock that made a real fix look empty, and test runs that could quietly pick up the wrong version of our code. We believe the numbers above are clean because of those fixes.
